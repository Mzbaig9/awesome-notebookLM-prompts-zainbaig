**Legal note on Bill 9, as it should be read.** Section 22 inserts s. 78.1 into the *Loi sur l'enseignement privé*: no agrément may be granted to an institution whose delivery of the educational services in the régime pédagogique, during the hours devoted to them, is based on religious norms or precepts, the transmission of religious convictions or beliefs, or religious practice, nor to one that practises segregation on religious criteria in selecting students or staff. Under s. 29, that first paragraph took effect 2 April 2026, which is why no new agrément can be granted to a non‑conforming institution since that date. The second paragraph of s. 78.1 (revocation under s. 123) and s. 23 (the minister may revoke on his own initiative) take effect 2 April 2029. The law does not prohibit religious education, does not touch permits as such, and does not reach unsubsidized schools through this mechanism. The proposal states exactly this and nothing more. Two things were not verified and are deliberately absent from the proposal: whether the law invokes the notwithstanding clauses, and the number of subsidized schools that would fall under s. 78.1. Government press releases mention gradual withdrawal of subsidies and a transition provision; I did not confirm the mechanics in the text, so the proposal stays with the revocation power and its date.

**Not verified, not used.** Official counts of private schools by permit and agrément status (available from the Ministère's directories; pull these during Phase 1). Any claim that no new agrément has been granted since 2008 (it appears in a FEEP brief on the earlier bill but I did not read it in the source). Laval faculty names; the proposal names no supervisor.

## Part III. Research design and feasibility

**Population.** Nonprofit private institutions holding a permit under the LEP, offering preschool and primary education, principally in Montréal. FEEP reports its members are almost all nonprofits, so the nonprofit criterion excludes little; the real filter is a documented distinctive project. Phase 1 screens 60 to 100 schools from the Ministère's school directory and institutional websites. If the Montréal primary population with a clear distinctive project is thinner than expected for one cell (Waldorf is the likely bottleneck), extend that cell to other regions before extending to secondary.

**Why primary only.** Subsidy rules, program obligations, selection practices and governance weight differ markedly between primary and secondary, and Montessori and Waldorf schools appear to concentrate at the lower levels (confirm during Phase 1). Restricting to primary keeps the comparison about mission type and revenue structure, not about level of schooling.

**Sampling.** Purposive, 12 to 16 cases on a grid of four mission types (confessional; Montessori; Waldorf or other alternative; bilingual or international) crossed with subsidized versus unsubsidized, then varied on size, age and founder‑led versus formalized governance. Not every cell will exist (subsidized Waldorf may have no instance); empty cells are a finding about the field and should be reported, not forced. Within confessional schools, include more than one tradition so that "religious" is not a proxy for one community. Classify by mission, not label: a school with a bilingual program but a confessional mission is coded confessional.

**Data needed.** Governance: bylaws or general descriptions of board composition, committee structure, delegation to the director, frequency of strategic planning, founder roles. Finance: published tuition and fees, subsidy status, CRA T3010 information returns where the school or its foundation is a registered charity (public on the CRA charities list, revenue by source and expenditure categories, but sometimes consolidated with non‑school activities), voluntarily shared financial statements, staffing and specialized training costs for the distinctive model. Mission: founding documents, projet éducatif, strategic plans, websites over time (the Internet Archive allows a longitudinal view of how mission statements changed, which partly answers the old‑institution problem). Families: consultation structures, surveys, and how directors say they use them.

**Recruitment challenges.** The 2026 law makes confessional schools cautious about researchers; expect refusals and delays. Mitigations are an endorsement letter from the supervisor, a short plain‑language summary that states independence and neutrality, an offer of an anonymized benchmarking report to each participant, and over‑soliciting by roughly half. Board chairs are volunteers and harder to schedule than directors; plan for phone or video interviews. Founders of older schools may be unavailable; rely on documents and long‑serving staff and flag the evidence as retrospective.

**Fallback if financial records are closed.** Run the financial analysis on three public layers (fees, subsidy status, T3010 where available) plus a structured interview module in which the director or treasurer reconstructs revenue shares in bands (for example, tuition under 50 %, 50 to 80 %, over 80 %) and walks through two or three recent budget trade‑offs. This gives ordinal revenue‑dependence indicators and decision narratives, which is enough for the mechanism‑focused questions. What is lost is precise cost‑of‑mission estimation; the dissertation should state this rather than imply it.

**Threats to validity and comparability.** Self‑selection (schools comfortable with scrutiny participate, those in difficulty do not), which biases toward success stories; mitigate by recruiting on the documentary typology and reporting refusal patterns. Retrospective bias in founding accounts; triangulate with documents and date‑anchored incidents. Social desirability in mission talk; ask about concrete decisions, not values. Non‑comparable financial data across charity and non‑charity schools; use ratios and bands, not dollar amounts. Policy shock confound: interviews in 2027–2028 will be colored by the 2029 deadline for subsidized confessional schools, which is useful for Study 3 but must not dominate Studies 1 and 2; ask the same core questions across all cases. Researcher positionality, addressed by excluding any school with professional ties, a reflexive log and double‑coding a subset.

**Timeline.** Year 1 coursework, systematic review (to support or revise the gap claim) and Phase 1 mapping; ethics approval at the end of year 1. Year 2 fieldwork (Phases 2 to 4). Years 3 and 4 analysis and writing. An article‑based thesis fits naturally: Study 1 on mission construction, Study 2 on financing and resource allocation, Study 3 on adaptation, all using one corpus.

## Part IV. Critical evaluation

**Scientific originality.** Moderate to strong. The individual ingredients exist (mission content analysis, Tremblay's work on distinctive Montréal schools, funding effects in BC, mission drift theory). The contribution is the combination: mechanism‑level comparison of governance and revenue structure across mission types in one jurisdiction. The proposal does not claim a void, which makes the gap more credible, but a reviewer will want the systematic review to confirm it.

**Importance of the problem.** Strong. Private schooling serves roughly one in eight Québec students in general education, nearly all through nonprofit boards, and the sustainability of distinctive projects is a live administrative question regardless of any single law.

**Fit with educational administration.** Strong. The unit of analysis is the organization and its decisions; families and law appear only as influences on those decisions. It maps cleanly onto Laval's axes on organizations and institutions, planning and management, and policy analysis.

**Integration of finance and governance.** Good. Finance is an explanatory dimension with indicators and a fallback, not a description. The weak point is that "allocation of resources to the mission" is hard to measure without detailed budgets; the incident‑based interview design partly compensates.

**Theoretical coherence.** Good. Three frameworks with distinct jobs (logics for competing demands, resource dependence for revenue power, identity and drift for continuity versus change). Stakeholder theory is folded in rather than added. Risk: the frameworks overlap enough that the analysis could become eclectic unless the supervisor helps choose a lead lens.

**Methodological feasibility.** Fair to good. 12 to 16 cases and 30 to 45 interviews is realistic for a four‑year Ph. D. Recruitment in the confessional cells is the main risk.

**Research population and data availability.** Fair. Mission documents are public; governance details partly; finances unevenly. The design survives partial access, but precision will vary by case.

**Contemporary relevance.** Strong without dependence. Bill 9 gives a natural experiment for Study 3, while the question applies equally to Montessori, Waldorf and bilingual schools facing enrolment or cost pressure.

**Fit with Université Laval.** Strong on content. The admission dossier still requires written supervisor confirmation, which is the real gate; the proposal names no professor, as it should until someone agrees.

**Rating: 7.5 / 10.** A coherent, administratively centered and defensible project whose main exposures are data access and the breadth of four mission types.

**Three improvements that would strengthen it most.** First, secure a supervisor and adjust the theoretical lead to their expertise, since a proposal reads very differently when it speaks the language of the person who will sign it; ask them whether institutional logics or resource dependence should be the primary lens. Second, run a quick pre‑mapping before submitting (count schools per cell from the Ministère directory and websites) and replace "60 to 100" and "12 to 16" with numbers grounded in the actual population; that single paragraph of evidence answers the committee's biggest feasibility doubt. Third, consider narrowing to three mission types (for example confessional, Montessori, bilingual or international) if the Waldorf cell turns out too small, trading breadth for deeper cases and cleaner comparison.
