RESPONSE TO THE ICNA SHARIAH COUNCIL

Re: Request for Clarification, Proposed Murabaha Financing, Stearns Bank National Association, Islamic Finance Division, and ICNA Relief

Attention: Shaykh Abdur Rahman Khan, Chairman, ICNA Shariah Council

As Salamu Alaykum wa Rahmatullahi wa Barakatuh.

We thank the Council for its careful review and for the four matters it has raised. We answer them in the order in which the Council has put them. Where the structure is settled we say so, and where we consider that the documentation must be strengthened before the transaction proceeds, we say that plainly as well, so that the Council can weigh the position as it actually stands rather than as we would prefer to present it. The full set of documents is available to the Council on request and we would welcome the opportunity to walk the Council through them.

1. Ownership and Risk

The Bank acquires ownership of the asset through a purchasing agent appointed for that purpose, and it acquires that ownership before it sells the asset to ICNA Relief, not simultaneously with the sale.

The sequence is as follows. ICNA Relief first identifies the property, negotiates the purchase terms with the seller, and submits a purchase request to the Bank setting out the property, the seller, the original price, the total acquisition price, the downpayment and the profit amount. The Bank then appoints its purchasing agent under a separate agency instrument. The agent acquires the property from the seller for the account of the Bank and with the Bank's funds. Only after that acquisition is complete does ICNA Relief, now acting in its own capacity as buyer rather than as agent, submit its offer to purchase the property from the Bank, and that offer carries a confirmation that the acquisition and the taking of possession under the agency have been completed. The Bank then issues its acceptance, which conveys its interest to ICNA Relief and at the same moment terminates the agency. The Murabaha sale is concluded by that offer and acceptance and by nothing earlier in the sequence.

The ownership the Bank acquires is beneficial and constructive rather than registered. Registered title is conveyed by the seller directly into the name of ICNA Relief and the Bank does not take a deed. We state this openly because the Council is entitled to weigh it. What the Bank acquires and holds for the interval before its own sale is the beneficial interest in the property, acquired for its account by its agent and with its funds. The reason for the arrangement is not a Shariah consideration but a recording and regulatory one, namely the avoidance of a double conveyance tax and a double recording of title, and the difficulty a national bank faces in taking record title to real property. In the Hanafi position, and as reflected in the AAOIFI Shariah Standard on Murabaha to the Purchase Orderer, qabd may be hukmi, constructive, established by the passing of the asset into the buyer's disposal and liability rather than by physical seizure or by any particular register.

On risk, we accept without reservation the Council's premise that ownership without damaan is not ownership, and that profit taken on an asset the seller never stood behind is the profit forbidden by the words of the Prophet, sallallahu alayhi wa sallam, wa la ribhu ma lam yudman, there is no lawful profit on that which one has not borne the liability for, reported by Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi and al-Nasai, and by the maxim al-kharaju bi al-damaan.

The documentation allocates risk to the Bank for the interval during which it holds the property. Title and risk of loss pass to ICNA Relief only upon the Bank's acceptance of its purchase offer, and the Bank's disclaimer of liability is drawn to operate only from that point forward. The general indemnity given by ICNA Relief has been amended in the current draft so that events occurring at the property during the Bank's ownership window are carved out of it and left with the Bank. Property taxes and insurance attributable to the property are included in the acquisition cost and are therefore borne economically by the Bank as owner for that window, and the agent insures for the owner's account rather than its own.

We will not overstate the interval. The Bank's ownership window is short and in most closings will be measured in minutes on a single closing date. Brevity does not defeat it. What would defeat it is the Bank standing outside the risk during that window, and on that we consider that two provisions still read more broadly than the Shariah permits. The first is the indemnity given by the purchasing agent, which as presently drawn is a general indemnity against third party claims relating to the property and is not confined to the agent's own negligence, breach of mandate or wilful misconduct, nor carved back for the Bank's own ownership window. The second is an indemnity in favour of the Bank in respect of liens and of defects in the quality or condition of the property, which if read to reach defects existing while the Bank held the property would transfer to the buyer the very liability for defects that a seller must carry.

We have therefore required, and the Bank is prepared to accept, that the agent's indemnity be qualified so that the agent is liable only for its own negligence, breach of the agency mandate or wilful misconduct, this being the settled rule that the wakeel is an ameen and not a daamin, and that the defect indemnity be expressed to operate only in respect of matters arising after the Bank's acceptance, leaving pre-acceptance title defects and latent defects with the Bank as seller. With those two amendments the Bank's damaan for the period of its ownership is complete and unqualified, and we invite the Council to treat them as conditions of its opinion.

One further matter should be put before the Council rather than left for it to discover. The transaction includes a tax and regulatory agreement under which the parties agree how they will report the arrangement for United States tax and bank regulatory purposes, namely as a single financing, with ICNA Relief treated as the owner throughout and the profit reported as interest. That agreement governs reporting and filing, and it says so in terms. It does not govern the legal or the Shariah characterisation of the transfers, which are governed by the Murabaha documents, and the same agreement records that the transaction is intended and structured as a Murabaha compliant with the Shariah. Different bodies of law characterise the same real transfers differently, and a tax characterisation does not unwind a sale that in fact occurred. We raise it because the Council should have it before it now rather than later.

2. Agency (Wakaalah)

Yes. A Wakaalah is contemplated. It is documented as a separate instrument, executed before any funds are disbursed and terminated before the Murabaha sale is completed.

Its terms are these. The Bank is the muwakkil and ICNA Relief is the wakeel. The appointment is expressly limited to the purchase and acquisition of the property from the seller and the related transfer of title, and does not extend beyond that. The agent acquires for the account and benefit of the Bank, not for its own account. The agency is unpaid, the Bank being under no obligation to pay the agent any fee or remuneration for its services, which is a valid wakaalah bi ghayr ajr. The agent may not materially amend, waive or cancel the purchase contract without the Bank's prior written consent. The Bank may terminate the agency at any time. The agency terminates automatically upon the Bank's acceptance of the purchase offer, and that acceptance operates as notice of termination.

The two safeguards that matter most where the customer is also the purchasing agent are both present. First, the two roles are kept sequential and are separately evidenced, so that ICNA Relief acts only as agent until it confirms in writing that the agency has been discharged, and acts as buyer only after that. Second, the promise to purchase is unilateral. ICNA Relief promises to acquire the property once the Bank has acquired it, and no corresponding binding obligation is placed on the Bank, which retains the discretion not to proceed. This is the promise of one party alone, binding on the promisor, which AAOIFI permits. It is not the bilateral binding promise which we agree with the Council would reduce the arrangement to a sale of what the seller does not yet own.

We record our own preference, which we have put to the Bank and now put to the Council. The Shariah position is materially stronger where the purchasing agent is someone other than the ultimate buyer, whether the Bank itself, an affiliate, the settlement or escrow agent, or the closing attorney. Where an independent agent is used, the Bank's ownership window is evidenced by a party with no interest in collapsing it, and the objection that the buyer bought from itself does not arise. We would ask the Council to consider recording in its opinion that a third party purchasing agent is preferred wherever it is practicable, and that the buyer may act as agent only where the two amendments proposed above are in place and the offer and the acceptance are separately executed and evidenced on the closing record.

3. Murabaha Profit

The profit is a fixed sum, stated in dollars, and it is irrevocably determined at the time the Murabaha contract is executed. It is not a rate, it does not float, it is not indexed, and it is not subject to repricing or adjustment thereafter.

The profit amount appears in the purchase request, is restated in the purchase offer, and is confirmed in the Bank's acceptance. The Murabaha price is the acquisition cost plus that fixed profit, and the receivable owed by ICNA Relief is the financed portion of the cost plus that fixed profit. The documentation fixes the moment at which the price becomes irrevocable, providing that the receivable may be adjusted only up to the execution of the offer and the acceptance and not afterwards, and requiring that any transaction costs the Bank has incurred be added to the cost before that point, so that the total is complete and known to both parties at the moment of contracting. Prepayment carries no penalty and no termination fee, and any rebate for early settlement remains a matter for the Bank's discretion rather than a contractual entitlement of the buyer, which is the position we expect the Council will require on ibra.

The documents do define a floating benchmark, being the published prime rate, but it is used only in calculating the charitable amount described in paragraph 4 below. It plays no part in determining the profit and cannot alter the receivable. The Bank naturally has regard to prevailing market pricing when deciding what margin to quote before contracting, as any seller has regard to the market when setting a price, and that use of a benchmark as a reference at inception, exhausted the moment the price is agreed, is not the objectionable use.

Two points of confirmation are owed to the Council rather than assumed by it. First, the promissory note and payment schedule executed for this transaction must state a fixed obligation on their own face, whose aggregate is exactly the Murabaha receivable, and must not carry a variable rate. The Bank's standard forms are conventional lending forms which are amended for this purpose by the Murabaha documentation, and that amendment prevails over any conflicting term, but we regard it as necessary rather than optional that the executed note be fixed on its face and not fixed only by operation of an overriding amendment. We have made that a condition. Second, the Bank's right to recover increased costs arising from a change in law must not be capable of increasing the receivable. It is our position that it cannot, because the receivable is fixed at the moment of sale and such amounts fall within a separate category of supplemental charges which the documents confine to actual costs incurred and exclude from the receivable. We are prepared to see that stated expressly.

4. Late Payment

The documentation does not permit the Bank to profit from delay. It provides for a Taazeer charge that is payable to charity and never to the Bank's income, for the recovery of actual costs, and for the ordinary security remedies.

The mechanism operates as follows. On a default, including a failure to pay an amount when due, the Bank may, but is not obliged to, assess a charitable amount by written notice. The amount is a small fixed sum together with an amount calculated by reference to the published prime rate plus a stated margin applied to the sum outstanding, or alternatively the losses actually caused to the Bank. What is assessed is paid by ICNA Relief through the Bank to a charitable organisation or cause approved by the Bank's Shariah advisory board. The interpretive provisions of the Murabaha documentation convert every reference to a late fee, a late charge or default interest appearing anywhere in the Bank's standard forms into a reference to this charitable amount, so that no default interest survives anywhere in the transaction. The charitable amount is kept separate from the Bank's cost recovery, never forms part of the Murabaha receivable, and cannot increase the debt. Cost recovery for enforcement, collection, preservation of the asset and legal expense is confined by the documents to actual costs incurred.

That structure is the Taazeer and Tabarru mechanism the Council has in mind, and its basis is the ruling on the mumatil, the solvent debtor who withholds payment, of whom the Prophet, sallallahu alayhi wa sallam, said matlu al ghaniyyi zulm, the procrastination of a solvent debtor is oppression, agreed upon, and layyu al waajidi yuhillu irdahu wa uqubatah, the delay of one who has the means makes his honour and his punishment lawful, reported by Abu Dawud, al-Nasai and Ibn Majah. The charge is a deterrent against wrongful delay and not a compensation for the passage of time, which is precisely why it is stripped out of the Bank's revenue entirely.

We consider three refinements necessary for the mechanism to be sound in practice as well as in form, and the Bank is prepared to accept them.

The first concerns genuine hardship. As presently drawn the provision does not distinguish between the solvent debtor who delays and the debtor who is genuinely unable to pay. The Shariah does distinguish between them, and the command in Surah al Baqarah, that if the debtor is in difficulty then he is to be granted respite until a time of ease, is not discretionary in its principle. We have required an express carve out providing that no charitable amount may be assessed for any period during which ICNA Relief is unable to pay, as distinct from unwilling, and that reasonable respite be granted in such a case. We ask the Council to make this a condition of its opinion, and we note that it carries particular weight where the buyer is a charitable organisation dependent on donation income.

The second concerns the trigger. The provision as drawn permits assessment not only on an actual failure to pay but also on a state of affairs that might mature into a default. Taazeer attaches to actual wrongful delay and not to an anticipated one. We have required that the charitable amount be assessable only upon an actual failure to pay an amount when due, and that other defaults be addressed through the Bank's remaining remedies rather than through a charitable charge.

The third concerns administration and evidence. We have required that the recipient charity be an organisation unaffiliated with the Bank, that amounts collected be held in a segregated payable account and not recognised as income of the Bank for accounting purposes, that they be remitted onward within a defined period, and that evidence of onward payment be provided to ICNA Relief on request. We have also required an adjustment to the usury savings provision, which as drawn would apply any charitable amount exceeding the maximum permitted by applicable law to reduce the buyer's outstanding debt. So far as it concerns the charitable amount, such an excess should simply not be collected, since applying a Tabarru to reduce the buyer's own obligation converts it into a benefit within the sale.

Beyond that charge, the Bank's remedies on default are acceleration of the receivable, cash collateralisation of the amount outstanding, and enforcement against the mortgage and the other security, together with recovery of its actual costs. Acceleration is the calling in of a debt already fixed and does not increase it. The taking of security and of guarantees is rahn and kafaalah and is unobjectionable. We note for the Council that upon acceleration the obligation is the receivable then outstanding, and not that receivable increased by any amount referable to the passage of time.

Closing

In summary, ownership passes to the Bank beneficially through its purchasing agent and is held together with the risk of ownership for the interval before the Murabaha sale, subject to the two indemnity amendments described at paragraph 1, without which we would not ourselves regard the damaan as complete. A Wakaalah is used, separately documented, sequential and unpaid, terminating upon the Bank's acceptance, with our stated preference for an independent purchasing agent wherever practicable. The profit is a fixed sum determined and made irrevocable at execution, with no repricing and no indexation, subject to confirmation that the executed note is fixed on its face. Delay is addressed through a charitable Taazeer that never enters the Bank's income, together with actual cost recovery and the ordinary security remedies, subject to the three refinements at paragraph 4, of which the carve out for genuine inability to pay is the one we press most strongly.

One matter the Council did not raise but which we place before it for completeness is insurance. The property is required to be fully insured for the owner's account, with the cost forming part of the acquisition cost. Where Takaful cover is commercially available for a property of this character it should be used, and where it is not, we have proceeded on the basis that conventional cover falls under the general affliction that presently attends commercial property in this market, on which we would welcome the Council's own view.

We are ready to make the full documentation available to the Council, to answer any further question, and to attend a session of the Council in person or remotely at its convenience.

And Allah Knows Best.
